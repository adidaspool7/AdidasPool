\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cap:conclusions}

This chapter closes the report. It restates what the project contributed,
checks each objective against what was delivered, distils the lessons
learned --- including the experience of acting as Product Owner of the
two-person talent-pool subproject --- and sets out the limitations and the
future work that follow from them.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:concl-summary}

The work delivered the four contributions claimed in
\chapref{cap:introduction}, each backed by a concrete deliverable:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textbf{reference architecture} --- the onion-structured Next.js
	application described in \chapref{cap:design}, which respects \gls{gdpr}
	retention while preserving analytical value --- delivered and deployed to
	production.
	\item A \textbf{reproducible, auditable job-anchored fit algorithm}
	(\texttt{computeJobFit}) implemented as a pure function with the most
	extensive test coverage in the suite (\chapref{cap:validation}), with
	every scoring criterion individually explainable.
	\item A \textbf{dual-mode AI interviewer} (technical-skill validation
	and CEFR language assessment) integrated through a hardened HMAC token
	contract --- the integration layer being the author's contribution, the
	conversational service the second member's.
	\item The \textbf{project-management experience report} on the author's
	Product Owner role in the two-person talent-pool subproject of the
	BlendEd program --- delivered as the \chapref{cap:analysis} narrative and
	the reflections below.
\end{itemize}

\section{Goals Achieved}
\label{sec:concl-goals}

Measured against the seven objectives of \secref{sec:objectives}, the

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Centralise candidate data.} \emph{Achieved} --- a

	\item \textbf{Automate CV ingestion.} \emph{Achieved} --- the
	LLM parsing pipeline with provider fallback extracts structured

	\item \textbf{Provide job-anchored matching.} \emph{Achieved} --- the

	\item \textbf{Verify candidate proficiency.} \emph{Achieved} --- the

	\item \textbf{Empower HR communication.} \emph{Achieved} --- the

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:concl-lessons}

\subsection{Product Owner Lessons in a Two-Person Subproject}
\label{sec:concl-po}

Holding the Product Owner accountability while also writing most of the code
was the defining experience of the talent-pool subproject, and its lessons
cut both ways.
The advantage was immediacy: there was no translation loss between deciding
what mattered and building it. The danger was the mirror image of that
advantage --- when the same person sets priorities and implements them, the
backlog can quietly bend toward what is interesting to build rather than what
the client needs. Two practices kept this in check. First, the backlog was
disciplined to be \emph{client-driven by construction}: the MoSCoW-prioritised,
pain-point-traceable ordering of \secref{sec:method-backlog} made deferral an
explicit, defensible decision rather than an unstated one. Second, the
dedicated client-acceptance sessions that doubled as sprint reviews
(\secref{sec:method-scrum}) meant that the people who owned the problem signed
off each increment, which is the closest a one-person product role can get to
an external check. Within the team, the sprint review was also the moment the
second developer could push back on product decisions, since the increment
was concrete and demonstrable rather than abstract. The takeaway is
that combining the roles is workable at this scale only with deliberate
guardrails against scope creep; it is not a structure to carry into a larger
team unchanged.

\subsection{Architectural Lessons}
\label{sec:concl-arch}

The onion architecture justified its overhead at one specific moment: when
the AI Interviewer had to live in a separate Python process. Because the
Domain held no external dependencies and use cases never reached into
infrastructure, extracting the interviewer behind a typed token contract was
a boundary change, not a rewrite. The counter-lesson concerns the decision
not to use an ORM: the manual \texttt{camelize}/\texttt{snakeify} conversion
between database and domain was a real, recurring cost, and a later review of
the codebase showed it was also where type safety leaked (rows cast through
\texttt{any}). The cost was bounded and the strict compiler largely filled
the gap, but the experience is a reminder that removing a layer of
abstraction moves work elsewhere rather than eliminating it.

\subsection{AI Lessons}
\label{sec:concl-ai}

Three lessons recurred whenever a Large Language Model touched persistent
data. First, structured-output prompting beats free-form for any
value that must land in a column: validating model output against a strict
Zod schema, and tolerantly discarding invented values, was the difference
between trustworthy and unusable parsing. Second, LLM output schemas
must be versioned, because the shape the model is asked to produce evolves;
storing a schema version alongside parsed requirements let the system
re-parse only what was stale. Third, fallback providers are a
reliability mechanism, not a luxury --- the Groq-to-OpenAI fallback turned a
single point of failure into a degradation rather than an outage.

\subsection{Working with AI Coding Assistants}
\label{sec:concl-ai-dev}

AI was also used as a \emph{development tool} during the project --- for
bounded, author-specified coding tasks, the prompts for which are documented
in \appref{app:prompts}. Reflecting on that use critically yielded its own
lessons, and they were as much about the tool's limits as its leverage.

The first and most consistent observation was that the assistant does
not hold the whole project in mind. It reasons well about the file or
function in front of it but loses the wider context --- the layering rules,
the casing convention at the database boundary, an invariant enforced three
modules away. Left unguided it tends to solve the local problem in the
simplest way that compiles, not the way that respects the architecture; a
plausible fix would, for example, reach from a use case straight into
infrastructure and quietly violate the dependency rule the whole design
rests on. The practical consequence is that every output requires
careful validation against the design, not just against the compiler.
Generated code was therefore reviewed the way a second developer's pull
request would be --- checked for boundary violations, hidden assumptions and
silent scope creep --- and the strict TypeScript compiler plus the test suite
acted as a backstop rather than a guarantee.

The second lesson was that prompting is a skill that improved with
practice. When a prompt leaves the architectural constraints implicit ---
the target files, the ports, the expected shape of the result --- the
assistant has to guess them, and it fills those gaps with its own
assumptions rather than the project's conventions.
The prompts that worked, and that \secref{app:prompts-development}
records, share a shape that emerged over the project: state the architectural
constraints explicitly, name the files and ports involved, specify the
expected shape of the result, and reduce the task to a bounded piece the
author had already designed --- so the assistant implemented a decision rather
than making one. Asking for the smallest reviewable change, rather than a
whole subsystem, kept each output verifiable. In short, the value of an AI
assistant on a structured codebase lived less in the model's raw capability
than in the discipline of the instructions and the validation wrapped around
it --- the same lesson the product side taught about LLM features, applied to
the act of building them.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl-limitations}

The work has clear limits. The validation survey drew on a small sample from
a single organisation, so its results are directional rather than
statistically generalisable. Automated test coverage, though substantial
(527 cases, with line coverage between roughly 80\% and 100\% across the
domain, application, and authorisation layers), is concentrated where the
decision logic lives and is deliberately thin over the infrastructure
adapters --- the data repositories and external-service clients --- which are
exercised only indirectly through mocked ports. Bulk imports
are processed serially in a time-bounded background task, which keeps a single
run to roughly 100--120 CVs and means the 500-file batch cap cannot always
complete in one window without a durable queue. The AI-assisted ranking and
the CV and job-description extraction were validated for internal consistency
but not against external ground truth --- neither the matcher's ordering
against a recruiter's own ranking nor the parser's output against a labelled
corpus --- because the project had access only to scraped job descriptions,
not to real application data, and recruiter time in the closing phase was
limited; \secref{sec:val-limitations} records both. The remaining operational
limits surfaced during validation --- the Chromium-only speech path, the
absence of an OCR fallback for scanned PDFs, the free-tier LLM ceiling, and
the still-manual retention purge --- are detailed in
\secref{sec:val-limitations} and frame the future work below.

\section{Partially Completed Work}
\label{sec:concl-partial}

A few capabilities reached the platform in incomplete form and were
deliberately left partial; \tabref{tab:feature-inventory} marks the principal
ones as \emph{Partial}. It is worth being explicit about what exists, what is
missing, and why each was not carried further.

\paragraph{Structured improvement tracks (FR-11).}
The data foundation exists: when an assessment ends in a failing decision the
system auto-creates an improvement track with progress entries --- the
concrete target of the borderline branch of the lifecycle state machine
(\secref{sec:design-domain}). What is missing is the \emph{candidate-facing}
flow over that data: a guided view that turns the stored track into targeted,
navigable development steps. It stayed partial by priority, not technical
obstacle --- FR-11 was a MoSCoW \emph{Could} (\secref{sec:method-backlog}),
deferred once the \emph{Must} and \emph{Should} items consumed the sprints ---
so the remaining work is additive rather than structural.

\paragraph{Bias detection.}
This remained a placeholder --- a named seam for a fairness module rather than
a working capability --- for two reasons. It traced to no validated client
pain point (the backlog was client-driven by construction,
\secref{sec:concl-po}); and the core fairness risk is already addressed by
other means, since the assessment is advisory rather than decisive
(\secref{sec:design-hardening}) and matching is explainable per criterion
rather than an opaque score (\secref{sec:sota-matching}). A meaningful
fairness analysis also depends on a volume of historical decision data that a
six-month retention rule precludes, further weakening the case for building it
within this iteration.

\paragraph{Live transactional e-mail delivery.}
The communication module is complete in everything but its final hop. HR can
compose individual contact messages and rich-text promotional campaigns,
target them through candidate preferences and segments, and every send is
recorded as a typed notification in the per-candidate interaction history
(\secref{sec:impl-notifications}); the Resend provider is wired in behind its
port. What is left dormant is the act of handing each message to the paid
provider --- which requires a verified sender domain and, more decisively,
would incur a per-message cost a student deployment has no budget to carry ---
so the system routes around it with a copy-link fallback that lets the
recruiter send the prepared message from their own client. The feature is
partial only in this switch-flip sense; enabling live delivery is a billing
and domain-verification decision rather than further engineering.

\paragraph{Single-origin location scoring.}
The location component of the CV score measures proximity from one hardcoded
origin --- the adidas GBS site in Maia --- rather than from the site of the job
actually being filled (\chapref{cap:implementation}). This was a deliberate
simplification for a single-site deployment: with every modelled role anchored
to the same office, a fixed origin produced correct relative rankings at a
fraction of the effort of a configurable one. It becomes a real limitation
only the moment the platform serves a second office or a remote role, at which
point the baked-in assumption distorts the score. Lifting it to a per-job
reference point --- or disabling proximity for remote positions --- is a
contained change carried forward as future work (\secref{sec:concl-future}).

These items are taken up again as concrete directions in
\secref{sec:concl-future}.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl-future}

The most immediate future work is a small tracked backlog of maintainability
items: extracting the remaining interview routes behind a use-case layer,
replacing residual \texttt{any} casts at the repository boundary with typed
row interfaces, and pre-filtering candidates in SQL before the matcher to
scale matching on large pools. Beyond these, several directions stand out:
automating the time-based retention purge as a scheduled, GDPR-compliant job
--- paired with a consent-renewal reminder sent one month before a record
expires, so candidates can opt to stay in the pool rather than lapse by
default; making the location component configurable rather than fixed to the
single Maia origin (\secref{sec:concl-partial}); widening
the analytics widget catalogue so analysts can express more questions safely
--- and, extending that further, a natural-language analytics assistant that
lets a recruiter ask for a chart in plain language (for example, ``new hires
per month over the past year'') and have it generated automatically, lowering
the barrier to ad-hoc analysis while staying inside the same safe, constrained
catalogue; making the AI interview job-specific, so that HR can attach a set of
priority topics to each vacancy and the corresponding technical or language
assessment becomes available to a candidate as soon as they apply for that job
--- tying skill verification directly to the requirements of the role; adding an
offline transcription path (for example a Whisper-based fallback)
so the spoken assessment works on browsers without the Web Speech API; adding
an OCR fallback to the ingestion pipeline so scanned and image-only CVs can be
parsed automatically; moving bulk parsing onto a durable queue with a
dedicated worker so large imports are no longer bounded by a single background
window; enabling accent-insensitive deduplication through the PostgreSQL
\texttt{unaccent} extension; and
introducing organisation-level data isolation to make the platform
multi-tenant.

A distinct and higher-value direction is the \emph{empirical validation of the
AI-assisted ranking}. In a later phase, run by the client's recruitment and IT
teams on live application data, recruiters would hand-rank candidate pools for
real vacancies and their orderings would be compared against
\texttt{computeJobFit} through a rank-correlation measure such as Spearman's
$\rho$, with the criterion weights and thresholds then tuned to maximise
agreement --- the step that would let the tool's rankings be claimed as
validated against professional judgement rather than merely internally
consistent. The same exercise would yield a labelled corpus against which CV
and job-description extraction accuracy could be measured in
precision-and-recall terms.

\section{Closing Remarks}
\label{sec:concl-closing}

The project set out to help an HR team manage a structured pool of
candidates efficiently, compliantly and usefully, under the constraint of
GDPR retention. It delivered a deployed platform that automates CV ingestion,
matches candidates to specific jobs in an explainable way, verifies language
proficiency through an AI interview, and gives recruiters the communication
and analytics tools the original pain points called for. Just as valuable as
the software, for the author, was the experience of carrying a product from
client discovery through architecture, implementation and validation while
acting as the subproject's Product Owner --- a rehearsal, at a manageable
scale, of how
technical and product responsibility combine to turn a real organisational
problem into working software.

\chapter{Analysis and Methodology}
\label{cap:analysis}

This chapter turns the problem stated in Chapter~\ref{cap:introduction} into
an analysed, prioritised body of work. It first characterises the
stakeholders and the two personas TalentHub serves, then revisits the
client pain points and the instrument used to validate them, sets out the
language talent framework that the client brief explicitly asked for,
derives the functional and non-functional requirements that follow from
those pains, and finally describes the project-management method --- the
BlendEd program's Scrum process and how it was adapted within the two-person
talent-pool subproject, in which the author acted as Product Owner.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\section{Stakeholders and Personas}
\label{sec:analysis-stakeholders}

Four groups of stakeholders shaped the work. The \textbf{client} --- the HR
and Talent Acquisition team at adidas GBS Porto --- supplied the problem,
validated the pain points, and acted as acceptance tester through recurring
review sessions. The \textbf{end users} fall into two roles that the
application treats as first-class personas. The \textbf{academic
supervisor}, Prof.\ Nuno Escudeiro, provided methodological guidance and
assessed the work in its academic context. The \textbf{development team} that
built TalentHub was a two-person engineering team (Section~\ref{sec:team})
that carried both engineering and, in the author's case, product ownership.
This team was one of four subproject teams in the BlendEd program: alongside
TalentHub, parallel teams built an onboarding assistant, a
community-research tool and an employer-branding solution. The wider program
brought together four product owners (one per subproject, the author among
them), four developers, two designers, and law, human-resources and marketing
students, working under a coordinating professor and a Scrum Master who
facilitated the shared ceremonies across all four teams.

Two personas were used to keep design decisions grounded:

\begin{description}
	\item[Carla, the recruiter (HR persona).] An HR specialist at adidas
	GBS Porto who simultaneously owns a number of open roles and triages a
	high weekly volume of CVs. She needs to find, compare and contact
	candidates quickly, to know whether someone has applied before, and to
	justify shortlisting decisions to colleagues. She is the primary user
	of the HR dashboard, the matching screens, the notification tools and
	the analytics.

	\item[Tiago, the candidate (candidate persona).] A multilingual
	jobseeker who applies through the public portal, uploads his own CV,
	completes language or skill assessments when invited, and wishes to be
	kept informed of status changes rather than disappearing into a black
	hole. He is the primary user of the candidate self-service portal.
\end{description}

The two personas justify the role-based split that runs through the whole
system: the same application exposes different navigation, permissions and
screens depending on whether the authenticated user holds the HR or the
candidate role.

\section{The Language Talent Framework}
\label{sec:analysis-framework}

The client brief did not ask only for a tool; its first stated objective was
to \emph{``design a language talent framework that defines the critical
language profiles required across adidas Porto's service areas and
establishes a structured process for attracting candidates who meet them''}.
The framework is therefore a deliverable in its own right, and the software
documented in the rest of this report is the instrument that operationalises
it. This section sets out the framework; the chapters that follow show how
each of its pillars is realised in code.

\begin{quote}
\emph{Note on sourcing.} The service-area list and the broad observation that
adidas GBS Porto operates across roughly fifteen business languages with an
international workforce of about 22\% come directly from the client brief.
The finer-grained assignment of \emph{which} languages are critical to
\emph{which} service area is a reasoned synthesis by the author, informed by
the brief and by the public structure of a European shared-services centre;
it is presented as a defensible model to be calibrated with the client, not
as an audited inventory of adidas's internal staffing data.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Critical Language Profiles by Service Area}
\label{sec:framework-profiles}

adidas Business in Portugal delivers shared services --- Finance, Human
Resources, Accounts and Sales Management, Supply Chain, Digital Services and
Tech --- to other adidas markets across Europe. Each service area inherits the
language needs of the markets it serves, which is what makes language a
\emph{role requirement} rather than a generic nice-to-have. English functions
as the common operating language across every area; the additional ``business
languages'' attach to the markets a team supports.
Table~\ref{tab:language-profiles} captures this mapping as the framework's
core artefact: a per-area profile of the language capabilities that make a
candidate valuable, expressed in the same CEFR vocabulary the assessment
module produces.

which the language becomes an asset.}
\label{tab:language-profiles}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\linewidth}p{0.40\linewidth}p{0.28\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Service area} & \textbf{Typically critical languages} & \textbf{Indicative working level} \\
\midrule
Finance & German, French, Italian, Spanish & B2--C1 (written + spoken) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two properties of this model matter for the design. First, the relevant level
is rarely native fluency: most roles need a \emph{working} business level
(B1--C1), which is exactly the band a structured, CEFR-aligned assessment can
place a candidate into with confidence, whereas self-declared ``fluent /
intermediate / basic'' labels cannot. Second, the profile is a property of
the \emph{role}, not of the candidate in the abstract --- which is precisely
why matching in TalentHub is job-anchored (Chapter~\ref{cap:design}): a
candidate's languages are scored against the profile a given opening
requires, not against a universal yardstick.

\subsection{The Three Pillars: Attract, Develop, Retain}
\label{sec:framework-pillars}

The brief framed the strategy as three obligations --- to \emph{attract},
\emph{develop} and \emph{retain} language capability sustainably.
Table~\ref{tab:framework-pillars} maps each pillar to the concrete platform
capabilities that operationalise it, so the framework is not left as an
abstraction but is traceable to features that this report later implements
and validates.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption[Language talent framework pillars mapped to platform capabilities]{The
three framework pillars mapped to the TalentHub capabilities that
operationalise them.}
\label{tab:framework-pillars}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.16\linewidth}p{0.40\linewidth}p{0.34\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pillar} & \textbf{Framework intent} & \textbf{Operationalised by} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Attract} & Source and recognise candidates who hold the critical language profiles, and target outreach where the gaps are & CV parsing that tags languages; job-anchored matching against the per-area profile; ambassador programs; preference-aware campaigns; analytics on language supply \\
\textbf{Develop} & Verify real proficiency objectively and grow borderline talent rather than discarding it & CEFR assessment with grammar/vocabulary/fluency sub-scores; borderline flagging; improvement tracks; longitudinal assessment history \\
\textbf{Retain} & Keep a persistent, compliant view of capability and re-engage promising profiles over time & Deletion-aware talent pool; interaction-history audit trail; re-engagement campaigns within the GDPR window; status tracking across cycles \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pillar most under pressure from the regulatory context is \emph{retain}.
The six-month GDPR retention rule (Section~\ref{sec:sota-gdpr}) works directly
against sustaining a language-capability pool, because the institutional
memory of who speaks what is precisely the data the rule forces a team to
delete. The framework's answer is not to retain data unlawfully but to make
the most of the window the law allows: structured capture so that value is
extracted immediately, an audit trail so re-engagement is compliant and
documented, and improvement tracks so a borderline German speaker is
developed rather than lost. This is the precise point at which the strategic
framework and the deletion-aware data model of Chapter~\ref{cap:design}
meet.

\subsection{From Framework to Requirements}
\label{sec:framework-bridge}

The framework closes the gap between the strategic brief and the engineering
that follows. Each pillar generates concrete demands --- recognise languages
automatically, score them against a role, verify them objectively, develop
and re-engage compliantly --- and those demands reappear, sharpened, as the
functional requirements of the next section and as the validated pain points
of Section~\ref{sec:analysis-pains}. The framework is thus the conceptual
spine of the project: the pain points say what hurts, the framework says what
a sustainable answer must do, and the requirements say what to build.

\subsection{Detailed Specification of Core Requirements}
\label{sec:analysis-fr-detail}

The table above is intentionally a summary; the \emph{Must}-priority
requirements that form the core of the product warrant the fuller treatment
given here, each stated with its rationale, the principal actor and the
acceptance criteria against which it was signed off during client review.

\paragraph{FR-01 --- Bulk CV upload with automated structured parsing.}
\textit{Actor:} HR recruiter (and, for single uploads, the candidate).
\textit{Rationale:} pain point \#5 --- recruiters spend significant time
extracting basic facts from each CV. \textit{Behaviour:} the user uploads one
or many files (or a ZIP archive); the system extracts text, sends it to a
Large Language Model, validates the structured result and persists a
candidate with experiences, education, languages and skills.
\textit{Acceptance:} a batch of mixed-format PDFs and DOCX files yields one
correctly populated candidate per CV; malformed model output never creates a
corrupt record; and a per-batch progress count is observable while parsing
proceeds in the background.

\paragraph{FR-02 --- Persistent, searchable talent pool.}
\textit{Actor:} HR recruiter. \textit{Rationale:} pain points \#1 and \#7 ---
candidate data lives in spreadsheets and cannot be sliced. \textit{Behaviour:}
every parsed or manually created candidate is stored in a relational pool
that can be filtered by status, country, score range, language and source
type, and sorted. \textit{Acceptance:} a recruiter can isolate, for example,
``German-speaking candidates in Portugal with a score above 70'' in a single
view without opening any document.

\paragraph{FR-05 / FR-03 --- Explainable job-anchored matching and scoring.}
\textit{Actor:} HR recruiter. \textit{Rationale:} pain point \#3 and the
\emph{attract} pillar --- candidates must be ranked against a specific role,
defensibly. \textit{Behaviour:} for a chosen job, the system computes a fit
score per candidate from seven criteria and exposes a per-criterion
breakdown and an eligibility flag. \textit{Acceptance:} every ranking can be
explained criterion by criterion; the same inputs always produce the same
score; and a criterion the job does not specify is excluded rather than
penalised.

\paragraph{FR-06 --- CEFR-based language assessment.}
\textit{Actor:} candidate (invited), HR recruiter (reviewer).
\textit{Rationale:} the \emph{develop} pillar and pain point \#3 ---
self-declared proficiency is not trustworthy. \textit{Behaviour:} an invited
candidate completes an AI-mediated assessment that returns a CEFR level with
grammar, vocabulary and fluency sub-scores, stored for human review.
\textit{Acceptance:} the result is reproducible in format, never
auto-rejects, and is recorded against the candidate's longitudinal history.

\paragraph{FR-09 --- HR communication with audit trail.}
\textit{Actor:} HR recruiter (sender), candidate (recipient).
\textit{Rationale:} pain points \#4 and \#9 and the \emph{retain} pillar.
\textit{Behaviour:} status changes, contact emails and campaigns are recorded
as typed notifications and reconstructed as a per-candidate interaction
timeline. \textit{Acceptance:} any team member can retrieve the complete
interaction history for a candidate, including who sent what and when.

\paragraph{FR-17 --- Authentication and role-based access control.}
\textit{Actor:} all users. \textit{Rationale:} emerged once the candidate
portal and HR dashboard had to coexist. \textit{Behaviour:} Google OAuth
authentication with an immutable server-set role claim gates HR-only
operations. \textit{Acceptance:} an unauthenticated API call is rejected with
\texttt{401} and a non-HR call to an HR-only route with \texttt{403}.

\section{Non-Functional Requirements}
\label{sec:analysis-nfr}

Beyond what the system does, several quality attributes constrained how it
had to be built:

\begin{description}
	\item[Transparency.] All scoring and matching must be explainable.
	Decisions are produced by deterministic formulas with per-component
	breakdowns rather than opaque models, and the system only ranks ---
	it never auto-rejects a candidate without human review.
	\item[Security and compliance.] The platform handles personal data
	under \gls{gdpr}. Access control is enforced server-side; the data
	model is deletion-aware to support the six-month retention rule; and
	authorisation for HR-only operations is gated in middleware rather
	than in the client.
	\item[Reliability.] Correctness is protected by continuous integration
	that type-checks and runs the full test suite on every change
	(Chapter~\ref{cap:validation}).
	\item[Maintainability.] The codebase follows an onion architecture
	with a strict dependency rule and end-to-end type safety, so that a
	two-person team can keep changing it safely.
	\item[Cost and portability.] The system must run on free service
	tiers (Groq as the primary \gls{llm} with an OpenAI fallback, Supabase
	for database, authentication and storage) and deploy to a serverless
	host. The only hard portability limit is the browser-native
	speech recognition and synthesis used in assessments, which is
	available on Chromium-based browsers.
\end{description}

\section{Project Management}
\label{sec:analysis-pm}

The BlendEd program ran on Scrum, with a Scrum Master facilitating shared
ceremonies across its four subproject teams over the February--July~2026
internship. This section focuses on how that framework played out for the
two-person talent-pool subproject. Acting as the subproject's Product Owner,
the author owned the product vision, the backlog and its prioritisation, and
the relationship with the client; both members acted as developers. The
section describes how the framework was adapted at subproject scale and how
the backlog was ordered.

\subsection{Scrum at Program and Subproject Scale}
\label{sec:method-scrum}

By-the-book Scrum assigns three distinct accountabilities --- Product Owner,
Scrum Master and Developers. At program level all three were present: a Scrum
Master facilitated the shared ceremonies, and each of the four subprojects
had its own Product Owner. Within the two-person talent-pool sub-team those
accountabilities collapsed onto two people, and the adaptation kept the parts
that add value at that scale:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Roles.} The author held the Product Owner
	accountability for the talent pool: gathering requirements from the
	client, maintaining the backlog, deciding priority, and accepting or
	rejecting completed work. Both members were Developers. The Scrum Master
	role sat at program level --- one person for the first two sprints and a
	second for the final two --- so the sub-team carried no dedicated Scrum
	Master of its own and kept internal facilitation lightweight.
	\item \textbf{Cadence.} Work was organised in short iterations aligned
	with the academic calendar and the program's sprint boundaries, each
	delivering a demonstrable increment of the deployed application.
	\item \textbf{Ceremonies.} Beyond the program-level sprint
	\emph{planning}, \emph{review} and \emph{retrospective} run with the
	Scrum Master across all four subprojects, the talent-pool team kept its
	own lightweight rhythm: an informal \emph{daily} sync kept the two
	developers aligned, and a dedicated client-acceptance session
	(Section~\ref{sec:val-manual}) doubled as the subproject's sprint
	review, so that the people who reported the pain points also signed off
	the increments that addressed them.
	\item \textbf{Tooling.} The product increment was always the live
	Vercel deployment. Source control on GitHub, a conventional-commit
	history (\texttt{feat}, \texttt{fix}, \texttt{docs}, \texttt{refactor})
	and continuous integration gave each increment an auditable trail, and
	the meeting minutes (Appendix~\ref{app:survey}) record the ceremonies.
\end{itemize}

The clearest benefit of the adaptation was the fusion of sprint review with
client acceptance: because the client validated each increment directly
against the originating pain point, the feedback loop that Scrum prescribes
was both real and short. The clearest tension --- one person holding both
the Product Owner and lead-developer roles --- is examined as a lesson in
Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusions}.

\subsection{Product Backlog and Prioritisation}
\label{sec:method-backlog}

The backlog was kept deliberately client-driven: every item traced back to a
pain point, and priority was assigned with \textbf{MoSCoW}
(Must / Should / Could / Won't) as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:functional-requirements}. The \emph{Must} items formed the
core that had to ship for the platform to be useful at all --- the talent
pool, CV parsing, job-anchored matching and the CEFR assessment --- and they
map onto the four priority pain points (\#5, \#3, \#9, \#2) identified in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis-pains}.

Ordering was also constrained by dependencies between modules rather than by
priority alone. The talent pool and the candidate data model had to exist
before CV parsing could populate them; CV parsing had to produce
field-of-work tags before job-anchored matching could consume them; and
matching had to exist before shortlisting and analytics could build on its
output. The backlog therefore advanced in dependency order within each
priority band, which is why some \emph{Should} and \emph{Could} items (for
example analytics) were delivered only once their \emph{Must} prerequisites
were in place. Requirements that emerged during development --- notably
authentication and role-based access control (FR-17) and per-skill
verification (FR-16) --- were folded into the backlog and re-prioritised in
the same way rather than handled out of band.

\subsection{Module Boundary with the AI Interviewer}
\label{sec:method-boundary}

The single most important organisational decision was where to draw the line
between the two developers. The conversational AI Interviewer was carved out
as a separate Python \textit{FastAPI} service with a narrow, explicit
contract to the Next.js core: the core creates a session and issues a
short-lived HMAC-signed token, the sidecar runs the interview and returns a
structured evaluation, and the core persists the result. Because the
interface was small and well defined, the two members could work in parallel
with minimal coordination, and the sidecar could be developed, deployed and
tested independently. This clean seam is also what made the onion
architecture pay off in practice (Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusions}).

\subsection{Risks and Decision Log}
\label{sec:method-risks}

The main risks were tracked and mitigated rather than left implicit. The
dependence on a hosted \gls{llm} was mitigated by a provider fallback (Groq
as primary, OpenAI as secondary) so that a single outage could not stop CV
parsing. The risk of \gls{gdpr} misalignment was mitigated by a
deletion-aware data model and by storing only the inferences actually
needed. The risk of \textbf{scope creep} --- amplified by the same person
holding the Product Owner and lead-developer roles --- was contained by the
MoSCoW discipline and by deferring whole feature families (bias detection,
internal mobility) explicitly rather than silently. Finally, the risk of
integration drift between core and sidecar was contained by the small, typed
token contract described above.

Table~\ref{tab:risk-register} records these and the other tracked risks as a
register, rating each by likelihood and impact (Low/Medium/High) before
mitigation and naming the response. The register was reviewed at each sprint
boundary; no risk on it materialised into a project-stopping event, which is
itself evidence that the mitigations were chosen early enough.

\subsection{Deviations from the Initial Plan}
\label{sec:method-deviations}

Two deviations from the initial proposal are worth recording, both minor and
both absorbed without disrupting the backlog; both were surfaced and accepted
at the end-of-April~2026 situation-point review with the client. First, the
persistence layer was migrated from the originally planned Prisma ORM over a
Neon PostgreSQL database to \textbf{Supabase}. The driver was integration
economy: adopting Supabase folded the database, file storage and ---
decisively --- authentication (Google OAuth) into a single managed platform,
removing the need to build and secure a separate authentication stack within
the academic timeframe. Second, a public \textbf{welcome page} was added to
the deployed application at the explicit request of the adidas Design team,
providing a branded entry point for visitors. Neither change altered the
scope agreed in sprint zero --- a talent pool with communication
verification, deliberately \emph{not} a full \gls{ats} --- and both were
folded into the backlog and re-prioritised in the same way as any
emergent requirement.

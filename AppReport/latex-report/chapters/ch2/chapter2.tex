\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{cap:state-of-the-art}

This chapter situates TalentHub within the existing landscape. It first
surveys recruitment software and the gaps it leaves for a small,
GDPR-constrained team; then reviews the role of artificial intelligence in
hiring --- CV parsing, candidate--job matching and conversational assessment;
introduces the language-proficiency framework adopted for assessment; and
finally summarises the regulatory context that shaped the data model. The
review motivates the design choices defended in \chapref{cap:design}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\section{Recruitment Software Landscape}
\label{sec:sota-recruitment}

Applicant Tracking Systems (\gls{ats}) are the dominant category of
recruitment software, and over the past two decades e-recruitment has become
the default mode of hiring in medium and large organisations
\parencite{Holm2012Erecruitment,LaumerEckhardt2011}. At the enterprise end,
suites such as Workday and
SmartRecruiters \parencite{WorkdayRecruiting,SmartRecruiters} integrate requisition
management, applicant tracking,
onboarding and analytics across very large organisations; mid-market tools
such as Greenhouse, Lever and Personio
\parencite{Greenhouse,Lever,Personio} target structured hiring with strong
interview-workflow and collaboration features; and lighter products such as
Recruitee and Manatal \parencite{Recruitee,Manatal} aim at smaller teams, increasingly
bundling AI-assisted sourcing and ranking.

Two observations follow from this landscape and frame the present work.
First, the category is broad but its centre of gravity is \emph{workflow}
--- moving a candidate through stages --- rather than \emph{intelligence}
about the candidate pool as a persistent, analysable asset. Second, the
features that this project treats as central --- explainable, per-criterion
job matching; a deletion-aware data model built around \gls{gdpr} retention;
and an integrated language-assessment pipeline --- are typically either
absent, opaque, or offered only as separate enterprise add-ons. The platform
described here is therefore positioned not as a competitor to a full ATS but
as a right-sized talent-intelligence tool for a single multilingual HR team,
where auditability and compliance are first-class concerns.

\section{Artificial Intelligence in Hiring}
\label{sec:sota-ai}

The recent surge of capable Large Language Models, built on the transformer
architecture \parencite{Vaswani2017} and beginning with the
demonstration that sufficiently large models perform tasks from natural
instructions with few examples \parencite{Brown2020GPT3} and continued by
efficient open foundation models \parencite{Touvron2023Llama}, has changed
what is practical in recruitment tooling. Three sub-problems are relevant
here.

\subsection{CV Parsing}
\label{sec:sota-cv}

CV parsing --- turning an unstructured document into structured fields ---
has progressed through three generations. \textbf{Rule-based} parsers
(commercial engines such as Sovren and RChilli \parencite{Sovren,RChilli}) rely on hand-crafted
patterns and gazetteers; they are fast and predictable but brittle against
unusual layouts and multilingual content. \textbf{Machine-learning} parsers,
typically built on transformer encoders such as BERT
\parencite{Devlin2019BERT}, improve robustness by
learning to label spans, at the cost of training data and pipeline
complexity. \textbf{LLM-based} parsing prompts a
general model to emit a structured object directly. Its advantages are
flexibility across layouts and languages and near-zero bespoke engineering;
its risks are \gls{hallucination} (invented fields), \gls{schemadrift} (subtly malformed
output) and per-call cost and latency. The literature's consistent lesson is
that these risks are mitigated less by the choice of model than by the
validation and recovery machinery surrounding it --- a strict but tolerant
schema that coerces malformed output and a fallback path. It
is this third-generation approach that the present work builds on.

\subsection{Candidate--Job Matching}
\label{sec:sota-matching}

Two broad approaches dominate automated matching. \textbf{Embedding-based}
ranking encodes both the candidate and the job as vectors and ranks by
similarity; modern person--job-fit research is dominated by such neural
representation-learning models
\parencite{Qin2018PersonJobFit,Jiang2020PersonJobFit}. They capture fuzzy
semantic closeness and require little manual rule-writing, but they are
\emph{opaque} --- a recruiter cannot easily see
\emph{why} a candidate ranked highly, which is a serious limitation where
hiring decisions must be justified and audited \parencite{Raghavan2020}.
\textbf{Rule-based} fit
scoring evaluates explicit criteria (skills, experience, languages,
education) and is fully explainable, at the cost of needing the criteria to
be modelled. Hybrid systems combine the two. For a compliance-sensitive
recruitment setting, the ability to show a per-criterion breakdown and a clear
eligibility flag is arguably more valuable than the marginal recall of opaque
similarity --- the consideration that motivates the rule-based direction taken
in this work.

The explainability argument deserves emphasis because it interacts directly
with the regulatory context discussed in \secref{sec:sota-gdpr}. Under a
legitimate-interest or consent basis, a candidate may reasonably ask why they
were or were not advanced; an embedding similarity of, say, $0.83$ is not an
answer a recruiter can defend, whereas a statement that the candidate met the
field-of-work, seniority and language criteria but missed two of three
required skills is both defensible and actionable. A further practical
consideration is data volume: embedding-based systems reward large training
or reference corpora, which a single HR team operating under a six-month
retention rule structurally cannot accumulate. A transparent rule-based
function, by contrast, performs identically on the first candidate and the
thousandth, which suits the intermittent, campaign-driven nature of the
target workflow.

\subsection{Conversational Assessment}
\label{sec:sota-conversational}

A growing class of products (for example HireVue, Sapia.ai and
MyInterview \parencite{HireVue,Sapia,MyInterview}) conducts asynchronous, AI-mediated interviews and scores the
responses. The technical core is prompt engineering for an evaluative task:
the model must both conduct a coherent conversation and produce a structured,
rubric-aligned judgement. Such systems raise well-documented concerns around
fairness and transparency \parencite{Raghavan2020,BogenRieke2018}. These
concerns point to a clear design principle for systems of this kind: the
assessment should never auto-reject a candidate, but instead produce an
explainable, rubric-based result for human review --- the stance the present
work adopts.

\subsection{Structured Output from Language Models}
\label{sec:sota-structured}

Both CV parsing and conversational scoring depend on a capability that is
easily overlooked: extracting a machine-readable object, not prose, from a
generative model. Early practice coaxed structure through prompt engineering
alone --- few-shot examples and explicit ``answer only in JSON''
instructions --- which works but fails unpredictably, producing trailing
commentary, malformed brackets or invented keys. Two more reliable
mechanisms have since become standard. \textbf{Constrained decoding}, exposed
by providers as a \emph{JSON mode} or \emph{structured-output} feature, forces
the model's token stream to conform to a supplied JSON Schema, eliminating
syntactic errors at the source \parencite{OpenAIStructured2024}.
\textbf{Schema validation} treats the model as an untrusted boundary: the
emitted object is parsed against a strict but tolerant schema that coerces
missing or surplus fields rather than throwing, so that a partially correct
response degrades gracefully instead of failing. The broader lesson from the
literature is that the reliability of an LLM feature lives less in the model
than in the contract wrapped around it: a prompt that requests a typed object,
a validating schema at the boundary, and a bounded retry together make
generative output dependable enough for production use. It is this
contract-first approach, rather than any single model choice, that the present
work builds on.

For language assessment the platform adopts the Common European Framework of
Reference for Languages (\gls{cefr}), which defines a six-level scale from A1
to C2 and describes proficiency through can-do descriptors across distinct
competences \parencite{CEFR2001}; its 2020 companion volume refines the
descriptors, notably for mediation and spoken interaction
\parencite{CEFRCompanion2020}. Compared with test-specific scales such as
TOEFL or IELTS --- which can be mapped onto CEFR bands --- CEFR is
test-agnostic, internationally recognised, and expressed as behavioural
descriptors rather than tied to a single examination. These properties make
it the natural rubric for an asynchronous, software-driven assessment: the
descriptors translate cleanly into the sub-scores the evaluator returns, and
the resulting level is immediately meaningful to an HR team operating across
many languages.

\section{Speech Interfaces for Spoken Assessment}
\label{sec:sota-speech}

A software-driven language assessment that judges spoken fluency must capture
and reproduce speech. Two complementary technologies make this possible in
the browser without native applications or paid services. \emph{Speech
synthesis} (text-to-speech, TTS) renders the interviewer's questions as
audio; \emph{speech recognition} (speech-to-text, STT) transcribes the
candidate's spoken answer back into text the evaluator can score. The W3C
Web Speech API \parencite{W3CWebSpeech2020} exposes both as the
\texttt{SpeechSynthesis} and \texttt{SpeechRecognition} browser interfaces,
allowing a purely client-side implementation that incurs no server cost and
keeps the raw audio on the candidate's device --- a non-trivial privacy
advantage under the regulatory context discussed below.

The approach has a known and accepted limitation: the recognition interface
is fully supported only on Chromium-based browsers (Chrome and Edge), and
recognition quality varies with accent and microphone conditions. Server-side
alternatives such as cloud transcription services or self-hosted models
(for example Whisper \parencite{Radford2022Whisper}) remove the browser constraint at the cost of
infrastructure, latency and data-handling obligations. For a free-tier,
privacy-conscious context the browser-native path is an attractive
trade-off, leaving server-side transcription available as a fallback where
broader browser coverage is required --- the direction this work takes.
Where such an assessment is advisory rather than decisive, occasional
transcription noise lowers convenience but does not produce an unfair
automated decision.

\section{Architectural Patterns for Maintainable Systems}
\label{sec:sota-architecture}

A platform expected to outlive a single internship and to absorb features
that were deliberately deferred must be structured for change. The relevant
body of work is the family of \emph{dependency-inverting} architectures.
Hexagonal architecture, or ``ports and adapters''
\parencite{CockburnHexagonal2005}, isolates application logic behind ports
so that databases, user interfaces and external services become
interchangeable adapters at the edge. The Onion Architecture
\parencite{PalermoOnion2008} expresses the same idea as concentric layers in
which all source-code dependencies point inward toward a dependency-free
domain, and Clean Architecture \parencite{Martin2017} generalises the
principle into the dependency rule that inner layers must know nothing of
outer ones. All three share a tactical core supplied by Domain-Driven Design
\parencite{Evans2003}: model the problem with entities and value objects, and
keep that model independent of frameworks.

These patterns matter here for two concrete reasons. First, they keep the
LLM and database choices reversible --- an application that talks to a
repository port and an AI-service port, rather than to a specific database or
model provider directly, can swap a provider without touching use-case logic.
Second, they create a clean seam along which work can be divided: a
conversational-assessment capability can be built as an external service
behind a narrow contract precisely because the architecture already treats
external capabilities as adapters. It is this dependency-inverting style,
with a framework-independent domain at its centre, that the present work
adopts.

\section{Regulatory Context: GDPR for Recruitment}
\label{sec:sota-gdpr}

Recruitment processes personal data and therefore fall squarely under the
General Data Protection Regulation \parencite{GDPR2016}. Several of its
principles bear directly on the design. Processing requires a \emph{lawful
basis} (typically consent or legitimate interest for recruitment);
\emph{data minimisation} requires storing only what is necessary;
\emph{storage limitation} requires that data not be kept longer than needed;
and data subjects hold rights of \emph{access} and \emph{erasure}. In
practice, recruitment teams commonly translate storage limitation into a
fixed retention window --- a ``six-month rule'' is a frequently cited
example --- after which candidate data must be deleted. This single
constraint is the central design pressure for a platform of this kind: it
means a recruitment platform must treat
deletion-awareness, an interaction-history audit trail, and a candidate
self-service view supporting the right of access as first-class concerns
rather than afterthoughts. That is the stance the present work takes.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:sota-summary}

The review exposes a consistent gap. Mainstream recruitment software is
strong on workflow but weak on the three properties this project treats as
essential: \emph{auditable} candidate--job matching rather than the opaque
neural ranking that dominates current research
\parencite{Qin2018PersonJobFit,Jiang2020PersonJobFit} and that sits uneasily
with the transparency expected of hiring decisions
\parencite{Raghavan2020,BogenRieke2018}; a data model built \emph{around}
GDPR retention \parencite{GDPR2016} rather than bolting
compliance on afterwards; and an \emph{integrated} language-assessment
pipeline rather than a separate tool. Modern LLMs
\parencite{Brown2020GPT3,Touvron2023Llama} make the parsing and
assessment components feasible for a small team, provided their output is
constrained and validated \parencite{OpenAIStructured2024}, while CEFR
\parencite{CEFR2001,CEFRCompanion2020} supplies a recognised rubric for the
language dimension. The remainder of this report shows how these pieces are
combined into a single coherent product for a multilingual HR team.
